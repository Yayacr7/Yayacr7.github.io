Part A: Your money

1. Budget and emergency fund

A budget is just a plan for where money goes before it goes. One simple starting point is the 50/30/20 split of take-home pay: about 50% needs (rent, food, transport), 30% wants, 20% saving and paying down debt. It's a rule of thumb, not a law; in an expensive city, needs can be 60% or more.

An emergency fund is cash you can reach quickly, commonly 3 to 6 months of essential expenses. It exists so that a car repair or job loss doesn't land on a credit card at 25% interest.

Order of operations most guides agree on: small emergency fund → pay off high-interest debt → full emergency fund → invest for the long term. Exact thresholds vary by person.

2. Compound interest

Interest is the price of money: what you earn for lending it or pay for borrowing it. Compound interest means you earn interest on your past interest too, so growth speeds up over time.

Formula for a single amount: Future value = P × (1 + r)n, where P is the starting amount, r the yearly rate, n the number of years. $1,000 at 7% for 10 years: 1,000 × 1.0710 ≈ $1,967.

Rule of 72: divide 72 by the yearly rate to estimate how many years it takes to double. At 8%, about 9 years. It's an approximation that works best between roughly 4% and 12%.

Calculator: compound growth

Compounds monthly. Real returns vary year to year; a steady rate is a simplification.

The biggest lever is time. Starting ten years earlier usually matters more than picking a slightly better investment.

3. Debt and APR

APR (annual percentage rate) is the yearly cost of borrowing. Compound interest works against you with debt exactly as it works for you with savings.

  • Good vs bad debt is about rate and purpose. A low-rate loan for something that grows in value or raises your income can make sense. A 25% credit card balance for consumption almost never does.
  • Paying off a 20% card is a guaranteed 20% "return". Almost no investment reliably beats that.
  • Avalanche method: pay extra on the highest-rate debt first (cheapest overall). Snowball: smallest balance first (more motivating). Avalanche wins on math; snowball can win on follow-through.

4. Investing: risk, diversification, fees

  • Stock: a small piece of ownership in a company. Higher expected return, bigger swings.
  • Bond: a loan to a government or company that pays interest. Usually lower return, smaller swings.
  • Risk and return travel together. Anyone promising high returns with low risk is either mistaken or selling something.
  • Diversification: owning many different investments so one failure can't sink you. An index fund does this cheaply by owning a whole market (for example, the 500 largest US companies).
  • Fees compound too. A 1% yearly fee sounds small but can eat roughly a quarter of your ending balance over 30 years compared with a near-zero-fee fund.
  • Time horizon: money you need within a few years generally shouldn't be in stocks, because a crash could hit right when you need it.
Most professional fund managers fail to beat a simple index fund over long periods after fees. That's well documented, but it isn't a guarantee about the future, and none of this is personal advice.

5. Inflation

Inflation is prices rising over time, which means each dollar buys less. At 3% inflation for 10 years, prices end up about 34% higher (1.0310 ≈ 1.34), so $100 kept in a drawer will buy only about $74 worth of today's goods.

The real return is roughly your return minus inflation. A savings account paying 2% while inflation is 3% is losing about 1% of buying power a year.

Part B: A business's money

6. Revenue, profit and margin

TermMeaning
RevenueAll the money from sales. Also called "sales" or "the top line".
COGSCost of goods sold: what it directly cost to make or buy what you sold.
Gross profitRevenue − COGS.
Operating expensesRent, salaries, marketing, software: costs of running the business.
Operating profitGross profit − operating expenses.
Net profitWhat's left after everything, including interest and taxes. "The bottom line".
MarginAny profit ÷ revenue, as a %. Gross margin = gross profit ÷ revenue.
Example: a coffee cartSells $10,000 of coffee in a month. Beans, cups and milk cost $3,000 (COGS). Gross profit = $7,000, gross margin = 70%. Rent, permit and wages cost $5,500. Operating profit = $1,500, a 15% operating margin.

Markup is not margin. Buying at $60 and selling at $100 is a 67% markup (40 ÷ 60) but a 40% margin (40 ÷ 100). Mixing them up is one of the most common pricing mistakes.

7. Profit is not cash

A business can be profitable on paper and still run out of money. Why: profit counts a sale when it's made, but cash arrives when the customer actually pays.

Example:You sell $50,000 of furniture to a hotel with 90-day payment terms. Your profit report shows the sale today. But you paid your workers and suppliers this month, and the hotel's cash shows up in three months. If your bank balance can't bridge that gap, you're in trouble even though you're "profitable".
Cash flow is money actually moving in and out. Many failed small businesses were profitable when they died; they ran out of cash.

8. The three financial statements

  • Income statement (profit and loss, P&L): revenue minus costs over a period. Answers "did we make money?"
  • Balance sheet: a snapshot on one date of what the business owns (assets), owes (liabilities), and what's left for owners (equity). It always balances: Assets = Liabilities + Equity.
  • Cash flow statement: where cash actually came from and went over a period, split into operating, investing and financing.

They connect: net profit from the income statement flows into equity on the balance sheet, and the cash flow statement explains the change in the cash line on the balance sheet.

9. Break-even

Fixed costs stay the same whether you sell 1 unit or 1,000 (rent, salaries). Variable costs rise with each unit (materials, shipping). The contribution margin is price minus variable cost per unit: what each sale contributes toward fixed costs.

Break-even units = Fixed costs ÷ (Price − Variable cost per unit)

10. Unit economics

Unit economics asks: does each customer make or lose money?

  • CAC (customer acquisition cost): total sales and marketing spend ÷ new customers won.
  • LTV (lifetime value): the gross profit a customer brings over their whole time with you. Simple version: monthly gross profit per customer × average months they stay.
  • A common rule of thumb is LTV at least 3× CAC. Below 1×, every new customer loses money, and growing faster just loses money faster.
Example:A subscription box earns $15 gross profit per month per customer, and customers stay 20 months on average: LTV = $300. Ads cost $120 per new customer: LTV/CAC = 2.5. Workable, but below the 3× rule of thumb, so improve retention or cut acquisition cost.

11. ROI and the time value of money

ROI (return on investment) = (gain − cost) ÷ cost. Spending $2,000 on a machine that adds $3,000 of profit is a 50% ROI. ROI ignores time, though: 50% in one year is far better than 50% over ten.

The time value of money: a dollar today is worth more than a dollar later, because today's dollar can be invested. To compare money at different times, convert future money to today's value (its present value):

Present value = Future amount ÷ (1 + r)n. $1,100 a year from now, with a 10% alternative return, is worth $1,000 today.

NPV (net present value) adds up the present value of all of a project's future cash flows and subtracts what it costs now. Positive NPV means it beats your alternative; negative means your money does better elsewhere.
