Negotiation, simply

Negotiation is not about being tough or clever. It is mostly preparation: knowing what you will do if there is no deal, knowing what the other side actually needs, and never giving something away for nothing.

1. Your BATNA: the source of all power

BATNA means Best Alternative To a Negotiated Agreement. In plain words: what you will actually do if this deal falls through.

  • Job offer: your BATNA might be your current job, or another offer.
  • Buying a car: your BATNA is the same model at the dealer across town.
The rule: whoever has the better alternative has the power. If you have no alternative, you have very little leverage, no matter how well you talk. The fastest way to negotiate better is usually to improve your BATNA before you sit down, for example by getting a second quote or a second offer.

Also estimate the other side's BATNA. If the dealer has 40 of this car on the lot at the end of the quarter, their alternative to selling to you is weak.

2. Walk-away price and the ZOPA

Your walk-away price (also called reservation price) is the worst deal you will accept. It comes from your BATNA: if the other dealer sells the car for $24,000, paying more than about $24,000 here makes no sense.

The ZOPA (Zone Of Possible Agreement) is the overlap between the two walk-away prices. If the seller will go as low as $22,000 and you will pay up to $24,000, any price between those works for both of you. Negotiation decides where inside that zone you land.

Try it: find the ZOPA

You never know the other side's walk-away for sure. Your job is to estimate it from evidence (market prices, how eager they seem, deadlines) and keep your own private.

3. Interests, not positions

A position is what someone says they want ("I need $90,000"). An interest is why they want it ("I need to cover rent and I feel underpaid compared to my peers").

Positions clash. Interests often don't. If the company can't move on salary, it might move on a signing bonus, remote days, a title, or a 6-month raise review, which can meet the same underlying interest.

Classic example: Two people fight over one orange and split it in half. One wanted the juice, the other wanted the peel for baking. Asking "why" would have given each of them 100% of what they needed.

Ask: "What's most important to you here?" and "What would make this a win on your side?" Then listen more than you talk.

4. Anchoring: who goes first

The first number on the table pulls the final result toward it. This effect is called anchoring, and it works even on experienced professionals.

  • If you know the market well, consider going first with an ambitious but defensible number, and give a reason ("Based on three comparable roles at $95k to $105k, I'm looking for $102k").
  • If you don't know the market, let them go first. Your number could be far below what they were ready to pay.
  • If they anchor badly, don't negotiate off their number. Name it as out of range and re-anchor with your own number and reasons.
Where people get this wrong: an extreme anchor with no justification can make the other side walk away or stop trusting you. Ambitious, yes. Absurd, no.

5. Trade, don't concede

Never give something for nothing. Every move you make should be tied to a move from them: "If you can do X, I can do Y."

  • Make concessions smaller each time ($2,000, then $1,000, then $300). Shrinking steps signal you're near your limit. Equal or growing steps signal there's more room.
  • Trade things that are cheap for you but valuable to them. A flexible start date may cost you nothing and matter a lot to them.
  • Negotiate the package, not one item at a time. If you settle salary first, you have nothing left to trade for vacation.

7. Common mistakes

  • Not preparing. Most negotiations are won or lost before they start.
  • Revealing your walk-away price. Once they know it, that's where you'll end up.
  • Splitting the difference too fast. "Let's meet in the middle" rewards whoever anchored more aggressively.
  • Treating it as a fight. In most business deals you'll work with this person again. Be hard on the problem, soft on the person.
  • Forgetting to walk away. If you won't walk away, you're not negotiating, you're asking.
