01 Finance · Lesson 01 of 21

Your money

Personal finance is mostly behaviour, not maths. But the maths explains why the behaviour matters.

The 30-second version
  • Spend less than you earn, automatically: save on payday, before you can spend it.
  • Keep 3–6 months of essential costs as an emergency fund.
  • Pay off expensive debt first. Compound interest works for you in savings and against you in debt.

Know where it goes

You can't fix what you don't measure. Track every expense for one month before writing any budget — most people underestimate their spending by a wide margin, especially on food and subscriptions.

A common starting split is 50/30/20: 50% needs, 30% wants, 20% saving and debt payoff. Treat it as a rough check, not a rule. In expensive cities, needs alone can be 60–70% of take-home pay; that's a signal to work on income or housing cost, not a personal failure.

The one habit that matters most

Pay yourself first. Set savings to move automatically on payday, so you're budgeting what's left instead of saving what's left.

Emergency fund

Keep 3–6 months of essential expenses in an easy-to-reach, insured savings account. Aim for the higher end if your income is irregular or you support others. This money isn't an investment; its job is to stop a car repair from becoming credit-card debt.

Debt: good, bad and expensive

Debt is a tool. The question is always: does what I'm buying earn or save more than the interest costs?

  • Expensive debt (credit cards, payday loans, often 20–30%+ APR): pay it off before investing. Paying off a 24% card is a guaranteed 24% return — no investment reliably beats that.
  • Moderate debt (car loans, personal loans): keep terms short and avoid borrowing for things that lose value fast.
  • Low-rate debt (some mortgages and student loans): often fine to pay on schedule while you invest.

Two payoff methods

MethodHowHonest verdict
AvalanchePay minimums on everything; extra money goes to the highest interest rate.Mathematically cheapest. Always.
SnowballExtra money goes to the smallest balance first.Costs more interest, but quick wins keep some people going. The best plan is the one you'll stick to.

Compound interest

Compounding means you earn returns on your earlier returns. Growth starts slow, then accelerates.

Future value = Starting amount × (1 + rate)^years Example: $10,000 at 7% for 30 years = 10,000 × 1.07^30 ≈ $76,123

Rule of 72: divide 72 by the yearly rate to estimate how many years it takes money to double. At 8%, about 9 years. At 24% credit-card interest, a balance you ignore doubles in about 3 years.

Inflation

Inflation is prices rising over time, so each dollar buys less. At 3% inflation, $100 today buys only about $74 of today's goods in 10 years. Cash sitting in a 0% account is quietly losing value. That's the reason to invest long-term money, not keep it all in cash.

Credit scores (US)

Lenders use your score to price loans. The rough order of importance:

  1. Payment history — never miss a payment. Turn on autopay for at least the minimum.
  2. Utilisation — how much of your limit you use. Under 30% is the common guideline; under 10% is better.
  3. Length of history — keep old no-fee cards open.
  4. Credit mix and new applications — minor factors.

Other countries use different scoring systems, though paying on time and using little of your limit help almost everywhere.

02 Finance · Lesson 02 of 21

Investing

Investing is buying things you expect to be worth more later, or that pay you while you own them. The hard part isn't picking winners. It's not sabotaging yourself.

The 30-second version
  • Higher returns come with bigger swings. "Guaranteed high returns" is a red flag.
  • Cheap index funds beat most professionals once fees are counted.
  • Fees compound too: 1% a year can cost a fortune over decades.

Risk and return are linked

Higher expected return comes with bigger swings in value. There's no free lunch. Anyone offering high returns with "no risk" is mistaken or lying.

AssetWhat it isTypical role
Cash / savingsBank deposits, money-market fundsSafety and short-term needs. Loses to inflation over long periods.
BondsLoans to governments or companies that pay interestSteadier than stocks, lower long-run return.
StocksPart-ownership of companiesLong-term growth. Can fall 30–50% in a bad year.
Real estateProperty, or funds that own propertyIncome plus growth. Illiquid and concentrated if you own one house.

Time horizon rule of thumb: money you need within about 5 years shouldn't be heavily in stocks. A crash right before you need it can force you to sell at the bottom.

Diversification and index funds

Diversification means not betting everything on one company, sector or country. An index fund buys every company in a market index, such as the S&P 500, for a very low fee.

The uncomfortable fact

Over 15+ years, the large majority of professional fund managers fail to beat a simple low-cost index fund after fees. If professionals with research teams mostly can't do it, a beginner day-trading on a phone app almost certainly won't.

Fees matter more than you think

A 1% yearly fee sounds tiny. It's charged every year on your whole balance, forever.

Made-up example: $100,000 for 30 years at 7% (≈0.05% index fund) → about $761,000 at 6% (same, minus 1% fee) → about $574,000 The 1% fee cost ≈ $187,000

Retirement accounts (US examples)

  • 401(k) employer match: some employers add money when you save for retirement. The rules vary by employer and plan: how much they match, up to what limit, and how long you must stay before the money is fully yours (called vesting). If your plan has a match, contributing at least enough to get all of it is usually the first step, because a 50% or 100% match is an instant 50–100% return on that money. Check your own plan's rules.
  • Traditional vs Roth: Traditional gives a tax break now and you pay tax later; Roth is taxed now and grows tax-free. Roth tends to suit people who expect a higher tax rate later.
  • Other countries have their own versions (UK ISA/SIPP, Canada TFSA/RRSP, Australia super). The logic is similar: use tax-advantaged accounts first.

A sensible order of operations

  1. Small starter emergency fund.
  2. Get any employer match your plan offers.
  3. Pay off high-interest debt.
  4. Full emergency fund (3–6 months).
  5. Max tax-advantaged accounts with low-cost diversified funds.
  6. Then taxable investing, extra mortgage payments, and other goals.

Red flags

  • "Guaranteed" high returns.
  • Pressure to decide today.
  • Returns that are suspiciously smooth.
  • You can't explain how the money is made.
  • Income depends on recruiting others.
  • Influencers paid to promote coins or courses.

04 Business · Lesson 04 of 21

How a business makes money

Every business, from a lemonade stand to Apple, comes down to: sell something for more than it costs, and don't run out of cash on the way.

The 30-second version
  • Margin = profit ÷ price. Markup = profit ÷ cost. They are not the same.
  • Break-even = fixed costs ÷ what each sale contributes.
  • Profit is not cash. Watch the bank balance.

The profit ladder

Revenue (what customers paid) − Cost of goods sold (COGS) (direct cost of what you sold) = Gross profit − Operating expenses (rent, salaries, marketing, software) = Operating profit − Interest and taxes = Net profit

Margin vs markup (people mix these up constantly)

Say a product costs you $60 and you sell it for $100.

Markup = profit ÷ cost = 40 ÷ 60 = 66.7% Margin = profit ÷ price = 40 ÷ 100 = 40%

Same deal, very different numbers. Always ask which one someone means. Margin can never exceed 100%; markup can.

Fixed costs, variable costs and break-even

  • Fixed costs stay the same however much you sell (rent, base salaries, software).
  • Variable costs rise with each unit (materials, shipping, payment fees).
  • Contribution margin = price − variable cost per unit. It's what each sale contributes towards fixed costs.
Break-even units = Fixed costs ÷ (Price − Variable cost per unit) Example: $5,000/month fixed, $25 price, $15 variable = 5,000 ÷ 10 = 500 units a month just to break even

Unit economics

Does each customer make you money? Two numbers answer that:

  • CAC (customer acquisition cost): total sales and marketing spend ÷ new customers.
  • LTV (lifetime value): average revenue per month × gross margin × months a customer stays.

A common guideline is LTV at least 3× CAC, and earning back CAC within about 12 months. If LTV is below CAC, growing faster just loses money faster.

Cash flow is not profit

Profit is an accounting idea; cash is what pays the bills. You can be profitable on paper and still go bust if:

  • Customers pay in 60 days but suppliers want payment in 30.
  • Stock (inventory) sits on shelves tying up cash.
  • Fast growth forces you to buy materials before customers pay.
The classic killer

Lack of cash, not lack of profit, is behind a large share of small-business failures. Watch your bank balance weekly and forecast it 13 weeks ahead.

Pricing

  • Cost-plus: cost + a markup. Simple, but ignores what customers would pay.
  • Value-based: price based on the value to the customer. Harder, usually more profitable.
  • Why price is powerful: on a 20% margin, a 5% price increase (if volume holds) raises profit by 25%. Most small businesses underprice.

05 Business · Lesson 05 of 21

Financial statements

Three reports tell you almost everything about a company's health. Learn to read them and you can judge any business, including your own.

The 30-second version
  • Income statement: did we make money?
  • Balance sheet: what we own and owe. Assets = liabilities + equity.
  • Cash flow statement: where the cash actually went.

Income statement — "Did we make money this period?"

Revenue, minus costs, equals profit, over a period such as a quarter or year. It's the profit ladder from Lesson 3.

Balance sheet — "What do we own and owe right now?"

Assets = Liabilities + Equity
  • Assets: cash, money owed by customers (receivables), inventory, equipment.
  • Liabilities: money owed to others, such as loans, supplier bills (payables) and taxes due.
  • Equity: what's left for the owners.

It always balances, because everything the company owns was paid for either with borrowed money or with owners' money.

Cash flow statement — "Where did the cash actually go?"

  • Operating: cash from running the business. This is the most important one.
  • Investing: buying or selling equipment, property or other businesses.
  • Financing: borrowing, repaying loans, raising equity, paying dividends.

A healthy mature business usually generates positive operating cash flow. Positive profit with negative operating cash flow year after year is a warning sign.

Ratios worth knowing

RatioFormulaTells you
Gross marginGross profit ÷ RevenueHow much each sale leaves after direct costs
Net marginNet profit ÷ RevenueWhat's left after everything
Current ratioCurrent assets ÷ Current liabilitiesCan it pay bills due in the next year? Below 1 is a caution sign.
Debt-to-equityTotal liabilities ÷ EquityHow much the business relies on borrowing
Return on equity (ROE)Net profit ÷ EquityHow well it uses owners' money
Context beats rules

"Good" ratios vary a lot by industry. A supermarket with a 2–3% net margin can be excellent; a software company at 3% might be struggling. Compare a business against its own history and against similar companies, not against a universal number.

03 Finance · Lesson 03 of 21

Funding & valuation

Where the money to start or grow a business comes from, what it costs you, and how people decide what a business is worth.

The 30-second version
  • You can fund a business from savings, a loan, or by selling shares. Each costs you something different.
  • An investor's share = their money ÷ the value after they invest.
  • Money tomorrow is worth less than money today.

Three ways to fund a business

SourceYou give upGood when
Bootstrapping (own savings, revenue)SpeedYou can grow from customer money. You keep full control.
Debt (loans, credit lines)Fixed repayments plus interest; often a personal guaranteeCash flow is predictable enough to cover repayments.
Equity (angels, venture capital)Ownership and some controlThe business can grow very large, very fast, and needs money to do it.
Reality check

Most businesses never raise venture capital and shouldn't try. VCs need a few investments to return their whole fund, so they push for huge, risky growth. That's often bad for a business that could be a very good, profitable, smaller company.

Dilution

Pre-money valuation = value before new money comes in Post-money valuation = pre-money + investment Investor's share = investment ÷ post-money Example: raise $250,000 at $1,000,000 pre-money Post-money = $1,250,000 → investor owns 250k ÷ 1.25M = 20%

A smaller slice of a bigger pie can be worth more, but only if the money actually makes the pie bigger.

Time value of money

A dollar today is worth more than a dollar next year, because today's dollar can be invested and because the future is uncertain. To compare money at different times, discount future amounts back to today:

Present value = Future amount ÷ (1 + rate)^years Made-up example: $1,000 received in 3 years at a 10% rate = 1,000 ÷ 1.1^3 ≈ $751.31 today

How businesses are valued

  • Multiples: value = a profit or revenue figure × a multiple seen in similar businesses. Small private businesses often sell for roughly 2–5× their yearly owner earnings; it varies widely.
  • Discounted cash flow (DCF): forecast future cash flows and discount each one to today. It's correct in theory but very sensitive to assumptions. Small changes to the growth or discount rate swing the answer enormously.
  • Assets: what the things it owns would sell for. Mainly used as a floor, or for asset-heavy businesses.

Honest caveat: valuation is part maths, part negotiation. A business is worth what a buyer will actually pay, and that links straight to the next lesson.

06 Business · Lesson 06 of 21

Why businesses exist, and what's changing now

A business exists to solve a problem people will pay to have solved. Profit is the signal that you solved it well. The tools for doing that are changing fast.

The 30-second version
  • Businesses exist because people pay to have problems solved.
  • Profit is the reward for solving a problem better or cheaper than the alternatives.
  • Today, one person with cheap online tools can start what once needed a team.

The main reason a business exists

A problem people have + a solution they'll pay for + a way to deliver it for less than they pay = a business

If any part is missing, it's a hobby or a charity, not a business. No real problem: nobody buys. Nobody will pay: no income. Costs more to deliver than they pay: every sale loses money.

Who a business serves

  • Customers get a problem solved.
  • Owners and investors get a return for the risk and money they put in.
  • Workers and suppliers get paid.
  • The community gets jobs, products and taxes.

The businesses that last keep all four reasonably happy. Squeezing one group hard usually comes back to bite.

What's changing now

ChangeWhat it means for you
AI toolsWriting, design, research and coding help are cheap or free, so one person can do the work of a small team. Your competitors have the same tools, so your edge is knowing your customer.
Audience firstMany new businesses start by building an audience online, then selling to it. Trust comes before the sale.
Solo and small businessesOnline stores, payment links and website builders mean you can start with very little money.
SubscriptionsSteady monthly income is attractive, but people now cancel subscriptions they don't use. You must keep earning it.
Reviews and trustBuyers check reviews before they buy. Honest, real proof matters more than ever.
Straight talk

Trends change quickly, and some of these will look different in a year. The rule underneath doesn't change: solve a real problem, for people who'll pay, for less than they pay. New tools make that faster, not optional.

Try it

Write your idea in the formula above: the problem, the solution, who pays, and what it costs you to deliver. If you can't fill one line in, that's the part to work on first.

07 Business · Lesson 07 of 21

Buy back your time

Ideas from entrepreneur Dan Martell's book Buy Back Your Time (2023), explained simply. The big idea: your time is your most limited resource, so stop doing work you could pay someone else to do for less than your time is worth.

The 30-second version
  • Work out your "buyback rate": what an hour of your time is really worth.
  • Hand off tasks that cost less than that rate and drain your energy.
  • Teach once by recording yourself, so you don't have to explain twice.

1. Your buyback rate

Your hourly value = yearly income ÷ 2,000 working hours Buyback rate = your hourly value ÷ 4 Example: $60,000 a year ÷ 2,000 = $30 an hour $30 ÷ 4 = $7.50 an hour

Any task you could pay someone less than your buyback rate to do is a task to hand off when you can afford it. Dividing by 4 keeps it safe: you only buy back time when it's clearly cheap compared with what your time earns.

2. Sort your tasks: the DRIP idea

Type of taskMakes money?Gives you energy?What to do
DelegateLittleNo, drains youHand it off first (admin, scheduling, inbox)
ReplaceYesNo, drains youTrain or hire someone to take it over
InvestNot yetYesKeep time for it: learning, health, relationships
ProduceYesYesThis is your best work. Spend more time here.

3. Teach once: record yourself

Martell calls it the camcorder method. Next time you do a task you want to hand off, record your screen or film yourself doing it and talk through each step. That video becomes the training, so you never explain it twice.

4. Bring solutions, not just problems: the 1-3-1 rule

1 problem, stated clearly 3 possible solutions 1 recommendation: the one you'd pick, and why

Use it when a team member brings you a problem, and use it yourself when you go to a mentor or investor. It turns "What should I do?" into a decision.

Straight talk

When you're just starting with little money, you may not be able to pay anyone yet. Start with free time-savers: templates, recorded how-tos, AI tools, and saying no to low-value work. These are our simple explanations of Martell's ideas; read his book for the full version. Sharp isn't connected to or endorsed by him.

Try it

Write down everything you did yesterday. Mark each task D, R, I or P. Pick one D task and find a way to hand it off, automate it or drop it this week.

08 Business · Lesson 08 of 21

Using AI at work without the headaches

AI can save a small business hours every week. It can also quietly cost money, leak private info and make confident mistakes in front of customers. Six habits keep the good and stop the bad.

The 30-second version
  • Count checking time: AI is only worth it if writing plus checking beats doing it yourself.
  • Never let AI tell customers prices, rules or promises without a person checking.
  • Keep your best prompts saved, test them monthly, and never paste private info into free tools.

1. Count the checking, not just the writing

AI writes in seconds, but someone has to check it. If checking takes 20 minutes, that's the real cost. Track a task for a week: time without AI vs time with AI including checking. The AI savings calculator does the maths.

2. AI makes confident mistakes

AI can invent a price, a refund rule or a fact, and say it like it's certain. Rules that keep you safe:

  • Anything with a number, a promise or a policy gets checked by a person before a customer sees it.
  • Give the AI your real price list and rules to copy from, and tell it to say "I'll check with the team" when it isn't sure.
  • Start AI on inside jobs (drafts, summaries, ideas) before customer-facing ones.

3. "It worked yesterday": test your prompts

AI companies update their tools, and a prompt that worked last month can start giving different answers. Keep your best prompts in one shared place, like the prompt library, not in one person's chat history, and re-test each one every month.

4. Don't paste private info

Customer emails, phone numbers, card numbers, contracts and passwords don't belong in free AI tools. Check what the tool's settings say about saving and training on your data, use a business plan where possible, and run text through the before-you-paste check first.

5. If you build AI for clients: demo vs daily

What clients sawWhat to tell them up front
A perfect demo on social media"A demo works once. We'll test it on 50 of your real examples before you rely on it."
"It can do anything""This version does three things well. Anything else is a new quote."
"It never makes mistakes""It will sometimes be wrong. Here's how a person checks the important parts."

6. Fewer tools, connected

A new AI tool launches every week. Pick one or two, learn them well, and cancel the rest; the AI cost check shows what unused ones waste. Then cut the copy-paste work: many apps can pass AI results straight into your spreadsheet, email or customer list with built-in integrations or automation tools that connect apps.

Straight talk

AI changes fast, so tools and prices here will change too. The habits don't: check what matters, protect private info, measure the real time saved, and keep a person responsible for what customers see.

Try it

Pick one task you use AI for. Time it for a week with checking included, save the prompt in the prompt library, and write one rule for what must be checked by a person.

09 Marketing · Lesson 09 of 21

Marketing basics

Marketing is getting the right people to know you exist, understand why you're worth it, and buy. "The right people" does most of the work.

The 30-second version
  • Pick one specific customer, not everyone.
  • Say why you in one sentence.
  • Lead with the benefit; use the feature as proof.

Pick one customer, not everyone

"My product is for everyone" means your message lands with no one. Describe one specific customer so clearly you could find ten of them this week:

  • Who they are: "first-time landlords with one or two rental units", not "property owners".
  • What problem they have that they'd pay to fix.
  • Where they already spend time: groups, sites, events, newsletters.
  • What they use now instead of you. That's your real competition, even if it's a spreadsheet or "doing nothing".

A narrow start isn't a ceiling. Win one group, then expand to the next.

Positioning: say why you, in one sentence

For [customer] who [problem], [product] is the [category] that [main benefit]. Unlike [alternative], we [key difference].

Example: "For first-time landlords who dread tax season, RentLedger is the bookkeeping app that sorts every receipt automatically. Unlike a spreadsheet, it's ready for your accountant in one click."

That sentence is your positioning. Test it on a stranger. If they can't repeat what you do and who it's for after 5 seconds, simplify.

Benefits beat features

Feature (what it is)Benefit (what it does for them)
Automatic receipt scanningNo more shoebox of receipts in April
Meals delivered on SundayFive evenings a week back with your kids
24/7 supportYour site never stays down overnight

Lead with the benefit; use the feature as proof.

The offer is part of the marketing

What exactly do they get, for how much, and what happens if it doesn't work? A clear price, a simple bundle and a fair guarantee often lift sales more than a better ad.

Straight talk

The most common first-time mistake is buying ads before knowing who the customer is. Ads amplify a message. If the message is vague, they amplify nothing, at your expense.

10 Marketing · Lesson 10 of 21

Getting customers

A channel is how customers find you. You don't need all of them. You need one or two that work, done well.

The 30-second version
  • Your first customers come from people you can reach directly.
  • Pick one or two channels and do them well.
  • Own your audience with email. Ads come last.

The main channels

ChannelCostSpeedBest for
Direct outreach (messages, calls, your network)TimeFastYour first 10 customers
Communities (groups, forums, local events)TimeFastNiche audiences; help first, pitch second
Referrals and word of mouthLowMediumOnce people love the product
Short video and social postsTimeMediumShowing, not telling; builds trust
Content and search (articles, tools)TimeSlow (months)Long-term free traffic that compounds
Email listLowMediumTurning interest into sales; you own it
Paid adsMoneyFastScaling something that already sells
PartnershipsSharedMediumBorrowing someone else's audience

The funnel

Every sale moves through the same stages. Most businesses lose people at one stage in particular. Find yours and fix that first.

Aware → they hear about you Interested → they visit, follow or sign up Decide → they compare, ask questions, read reviews Buy → they pay Return → they buy again and tell friends

Rules that save money

  • Your first customers come from people you can reach directly. Don't wait for strangers to find you.
  • Focus on one or two channels. Five half-done channels usually lose to one done properly.
  • Own your audience. Social followers can disappear if a platform changes its rules. An email list is yours.
  • Ads come last. Spend on ads once you know your message and your numbers. Then they pour fuel on something that already works.
Try it

List 20 people who match your customer and could hear from you this week. Message 10 of them. Ask about the problem, not your product. Their words become your marketing copy.

11 Marketing · Lesson 11 of 21

Measuring marketing

If you can't measure it, you can't tell good marketing from expensive guessing. Five numbers cover most of what matters.

The 30-second version
  • Five numbers matter: conversion rate, CAC, ROAS, LTV and payback.
  • Likes and views feel good; sales pay the bills.
  • Change one thing at a time so you know what worked.

The five numbers

Conversion rate = people who did it ÷ people who could have CAC (cost per customer) = marketing spend ÷ new customers ROAS (return on ad spend) = revenue from ads ÷ ad spend LTV (lifetime value) = revenue per month × gross margin × months they stay Payback = CAC ÷ (revenue per month × gross margin)

Worked example: you spend $600 and win 12 customers. CAC = $50. Each pays $25 a month at a 60% margin and stays 10 months: LTV = 25 × 0.6 × 10 = $150. LTV:CAC = 3:1, the common healthy guideline. Payback = 50 ÷ 15 = 3.3 months.

Vanity numbers vs real numbers

Feels goodActually matters
Followers, likes, viewsSign-ups and sales from those views
Website visitsConversion rate of those visits
Email list sizeRevenue per subscriber
Ad clicksProfit after the ad bill

Test one thing at a time

  • Change one element (headline, price, image) and keep everything else the same.
  • Wait for enough results. A handful of visitors can swing by luck; small tests mislead.
  • Track where people come from with tagged links (UTM tags) and a simple analytics tool, so you know which channel earned the sale.
Straight talk

Benchmarks you read online ("a good conversion rate is 3%") vary wildly by industry and traffic source. Your own numbers, measured consistently, beat any average.

Run your own numbers in the marketing calculators.

12 Marketing · Lesson 12 of 21

Words that sell

The words on your page, post or message do the selling when you're not there. Clear beats clever, and the customer's own words beat yours.

The 30-second version
  • Say what they get, in words they already use.
  • Every message needs four parts: the problem, your promise, proof, and one clear ask.
  • Cut every word that doesn't help someone say yes.

The four parts of any message that sells

Problem: name the pain in their words "Tax season stresses you out?" Promise: what changes for them "Get every receipt sorted in one click." Proof: why they should believe you "Used by 40 local landlords." Ask: one clear next step "Start your free trial."

It works for a website, a social post, a cold email or a flyer. If one part is missing, people stall. No problem: they don't care. No proof: they don't believe. No ask: they don't act.

Headlines: specific beats clever

WeakStrongWhy
"Welcome to Fresh Bites""Healthy dinners at your door by 6pm"Says what they get
"Innovative tutoring solutions""Raise your maths grade in 6 weeks"A result and a time
"Best bakery in town!""Gluten-free birthday cakes, ordered by Thursday"Specific, believable

A good headline passes the five-second test: a stranger knows what you do and who it's for.

Use their words, not yours

The best copy is stolen from your customers. When you talk to them (see Getting customers), write down their exact phrases. If they say "I'm drowning in receipts", that line will sell better than anything you'd write yourself.

The ask: one action, verb first

  • One ask per message. "Follow, share, buy and sign up" means they do nothing.
  • Start with a verb and say what happens: "Get my free plan" beats "Submit".
  • Lower the risk: "Free for 3 days, no card needed" makes saying yes easier.
Straight talk

Never invent reviews, numbers or fake deadlines. It destroys trust when people find out, and in the US fake reviews and testimonials can break consumer-protection law. Real proof, even small ("my first 5 customers came back"), is enough.

Try it

Write your offer in the four parts: problem, promise, proof, ask. Read it to someone for five seconds, then ask them what you sell. If they can't say it back, cut words until they can.

14 Negotiation · Lesson 14 of 21

Negotiation

Negotiation isn't a talent you're born with. It's mostly preparation, plus a handful of habits you can practise.

The 30-second version
  • Your power is your alternative if the deal fails.
  • Decide your walk-away point before you start.
  • Trade concessions. Never give them away for free.

Step 1: prepare four numbers and one plan

  • BATNA (Best Alternative To a Negotiated Agreement): what you'll actually do if this deal fails. Another job offer? Another supplier? Staying put? Your BATNA is your power. Improve it before you negotiate.
  • Walk-away point (reservation price): the worst deal you'll accept. Set it from your BATNA, and set it before you're in the room.
  • Target: an ambitious but defensible outcome. People who aim higher tend to get more.
  • Their side: estimate their BATNA and walk-away point too.

The ZOPA (Zone Of Possible Agreement) is the overlap between your walk-away point and theirs. If a seller won't go below $8,000 and a buyer won't go above $10,000, the ZOPA is $8,000–$10,000. If there's no overlap, no deal is possible on price alone, so you'll need to change the deal itself.

Step 2: interests, not positions

A position is what someone says they want ("I need $90k"). An interest is why ("I need to cover my rent and feel valued"). Two sisters fight over one orange and split it in half. One wanted the juice, the other the peel for baking; they could have had 100% each. Ask why to find trades that cost you little and are worth a lot to them.

Step 3: anchor deliberately

The first number on the table pulls the final result towards it. That's anchoring.

  • If you know the market well, consider making the first offer, ambitious but with a reason attached.
  • If you don't know the market, let them go first, or anchor with research ("Similar roles pay $85–95k").
  • If they anchor extreme, don't counter off their number. Name it ("That's far from what we're seeing") and re-anchor with your own reasoning.

Step 4: listen more than you talk

  • Ask open questions: "What would make this work for you?" "How did you arrive at that number?"
  • Calibrated "how" questions push the problem back to them: "How am I supposed to do that on this budget?"
  • Label emotions: "It sounds like timing is the real pressure here."
  • Use silence. After you make an offer or hear one, stop talking. People fill silence with concessions and information.

Step 5: trade, don't give

  • Never make a concession for free. Use "If you can do X, I can do Y."
  • Make each concession smaller than the last. It signals you're near your limit.
  • Negotiate several things at once (price, timing, scope, payment terms, extras). Single-issue haggling is win/lose; multi-issue deals let both sides win.
  • Don't split the difference early. It rewards whoever anchored more extremely.

Common mistakes

  • Negotiating against yourself: lowering your ask before they've responded.
  • Revealing your walk-away point.
  • Accepting the first offer: it signals you left money on the table, and often leaves both sides less happy.
  • Treating it as a fight: you may work with this person for years. Be hard on the problem, soft on the person.
  • Not preparing a BATNA: walking in with no alternative is the single biggest weakness.
Ethics and self-interest agree here

Don't lie about facts ("I have another offer" when you don't). It's often illegal in contracts, it's easy to call out, and it ruins trust. You're allowed to decline to share information ("I'd rather not get into my current salary").

Negotiation scripts

Word-for-word starting points. Adapt the wording so it sounds like you — scripts read robotically are worse than none.

The 30-second version
  • Make your ask, then stop talking.
  • Bring results and market data, not personal needs.
  • Cut scope, not price.

Salary — new job offer

When they ask your expectations early:
"I'd like to learn more about the role before naming a number. What's the budgeted range?"

When the offer arrives:
"Thank you. I'm excited about this role and the team. Based on [market data / my experience with X], I was expecting something closer to [target]. Is there flexibility on base?"

Then stop talking.

If base is fixed:
"I understand. Could we look at a signing bonus, extra leave, a title change, or a salary review at six months?"

Raise at your current job

"I'd like to talk about my compensation. Over the last year I [concrete result with a number: grew X by 20%, saved $Y, took on Z]. Market data for this role shows [range]. I'm asking for an adjustment to [number]. What would it take to get there?"

Bring results, not needs. "My rent went up" is your problem; "I saved the company $40k" is theirs.

Buying something (car, equipment, services)

"I'm ready to buy this week, and I'm comparing it with [alternative]. What's the best out-the-door price you can do?"

Then: "That's above what I can do. If you can get to [number], we can sign today."

Negotiate the total price, not the monthly payment. Monthly payments hide a longer loan and extras.

Selling / pricing your services

When a client says it's too expensive:
"I understand. Which part of the project matters most to you? We could reduce the scope to fit your budget rather than reduce the quality."

Cut scope, not price. Discounting trains clients to push back every time.

Rent or a bill

"I've been a reliable tenant/customer for [time] and I'd like to stay. Similar places nearby are listed at [number]. If I sign a [longer] lease, could we keep the rent at [number]?"

16 Negotiation · Lesson 16 of 21

Getting people to say yes

People agree with you when they feel understood, see what's in it for them, and find it easy to say yes. None of that needs tricks.

The 30-second version
  • Listen first, then show what's in it for them.
  • Make the ask small and clear.
  • Treat objections as questions: acknowledge, ask, answer, check.

1. Listen before you pitch

Ask what they want and what worries them, then repeat it back in your own words: "So the real concern is the timeline, not the price?" People say yes far more readily to someone who has shown they understand the problem.

2. Make it about them

Your reasons are not their reasons. Translate every point into what they get.

About youAbout them
"I need this deal to hit my target.""This gets your launch done two weeks early."
"Our product has 40 features.""You'll stop doing the invoicing by hand."

3. Make the ask small and clear

A vague ask gets a vague answer. Say exactly what you want, and make the first step easy: "Can we try it for one month?" is easier to agree to than "Will you sign a year-long contract?"

4. Back it up honestly

  • Proof: a number, a result, a short example. "Three local gyms use it" beats "it's great".
  • Give first: share something useful before you ask. People like to return a favour.
  • Be consistent: link your ask to something they've already said they want.

5. Treat objections as questions

An objection usually means "I'm not sure yet", not "no". Answer the worry behind it:

Acknowledge: "That's fair, price matters." Ask: "Compared with what you're using now?" Answer: one clear reason, with proof. Check: "Does that cover it?"
Straight talk

Fake scarcity ("only 2 left!"), invented deadlines and pressure tactics can win one yes and lose the relationship. If the deal is good for them, honest persuasion is enough. If it isn't, it shouldn't happen.

Pitching investors, simply

An investor wants to know three things: is the problem real, can you win, and can they make money? Answer those clearly and you've done the hard part.

The 30-second version
  • Problem, solution, proof, market, why you, the ask: in 60 seconds.
  • Know your numbers: revenue, growth, margin, CAC and LTV, burn and runway.
  • Runway = cash ÷ monthly burn.

The pitch in six lines

1. Problem: who has it, and how much it hurts 2. Solution: what you do about it, in one sentence 3. Proof: customers, sales, growth (traction) 4. Market: how many people have this problem 5. Why you: why your team wins 6. The ask: how much you're raising and what it pays for

Practise saying the whole thing in 60 seconds. If you can't, it isn't clear yet.

The numbers they'll ask about

Revenue and growthHow much you sell, and how fast that's rising each month
Gross marginHow much of each sale you keep after direct costs
CAC and LTVWhat a customer costs to win vs what they're worth

What makes investors nervous

  • Not knowing your own numbers.

  • Huge market claims with no customers yet.
  • Not being able to say what the money is for.

Staying calm under pressure

Tough questions aren't attacks. They're investors checking whether you know your business. Calm comes from preparation and a few simple habits.

The 30-second version
  • Prepare the 20 hardest questions before the meeting.
  • Pause, give the answer in one sentence, then explain.
  • "I don't know, here's how I'll find out" beats a confident guess.

Before: prepare the hard questions

Write down the 20 questions you'd least like to be asked, and an answer for each. Most tough questions are predictable: your numbers (especially your runway and burn rate), your competition, your biggest risk, why you, and why now. Practise them out loud with a timer, using the Investor pressure drill.

During: five habits

  1. Pause. Two seconds of silence before answering looks thoughtful, not lost.
  2. Answer first, then explain. Lead with the answer in one sentence, then give one or two reasons. Rambling is what pressure looks like.
  3. Clarify if you need to. "Do you mean this year's numbers or the forecast?" buys time and avoids answering the wrong question.
  4. Say "I don't know" well. "I don't know that number exactly. It's around X, and I'll send the exact figure tomorrow." Then actually send it. Guessing confidently and being wrong is far worse.
  5. Steer back to your strengths. Answer the question honestly, then connect it to something you're confident about: "That's a real risk, and here's how we're testing it."

In your body

Box breathing (before you walk in, or during a pause): breathe in 4 seconds → hold 4 → breathe out 4 → hold 4 Repeat 3 times.

Slow your speaking pace a little more than feels natural. Nerves speed you up; slower sounds sure.

Hostile or unfair questions

Stay polite and specific. Acknowledge the concern, answer with facts, and don't argue tone. "Fair challenge. Here's what our data shows…" You're also judging whether you want this investor. How they treat you now is how they'll treat you when things go wrong.

Straight talk

No technique replaces knowing your numbers. Most pressure in investor meetings comes from not being sure of your own business. Learn the numbers cold, and most of the nerves go with it.

Your voice in the room

Investors judge how you say it as well as what you say. A calm, clear voice makes the same numbers sound more believable. It's a skill, and you can practise it.

The 30-second version
  • Speak slower than feels natural, and pause on purpose.
  • End sentences with your voice going down, not up, so they sound sure.
  • Record yourself, watch it back, fix one thing at a time.

Five voice habits

  1. Slow down. Nerves speed you up. Slower sounds confident and gives you time to think.
  2. Pause instead of "um". A one-second silence sounds thoughtful. Fillers like "um", "like" and "basically" sound unsure.
  3. Land your sentences. Let your voice drop at the end of a statement. Rising at the end makes facts sound like questions.
  4. Breathe from your belly. Short chest breaths make your voice thin and shaky. Breathe in slowly before you start.
  5. Stress the number. "We grew 40% last quarter." Leaning on the key word tells them what matters.

Your body speaks too

DoAvoid
Sit or stand tall, feet flatSlouching or rocking
Look at the person who asked, then the othersStaring at your slides or notes
Calm hands that show size or stepsFidgeting with a pen or phone
A real smile when you meet themA frozen, nervous face

On a video call: put the camera at eye level, look at the lens when you make your key point, and sit facing a window or lamp so they can see your face.

How to practise (15 minutes a day)

1. Record your 60-second pitch on your phone. 2. Watch it once with the sound off: check your body. 3. Watch it with the sound on: count your "um"s. 4. Fix ONE thing, record again. 5. Then pitch it to a friend and ask: "What do you remember?"
Straight talk

Nobody expects a perfect speaker. Investors expect you to know your business and tell the truth. A slightly nervous founder who knows their numbers beats a smooth talker who doesn't.

Try it

Record your one-sentence pitch three times today: once fast, once slow, once slow with a pause before your best number. Listen back. The third one almost always sounds the most convincing.

Getting an investor interested

Investors see far more pitches than they can fund. You have seconds to make them want the meeting, and the meeting to make them want the next one.

The 30-second version
  • Lead with your strongest proof: real customers, sales or growth.
  • A warm introduction from someone they trust beats a cold email.
  • Pick investors who back businesses like yours, and follow up with progress.

What makes an investor lean in

SignalExample
Traction (proof people want it)"140 paying customers in 4 months, most found us by word of mouth."
A big, painful problem"Small landlords lose a weekend every tax season."
Why you"I ran rentals for 5 years and built this to fix my own problem."
Why now"New rules this year force every landlord to keep digital records."

Your one-line hook

We help [who] [get what result], and we've already [your best proof].

Example: "We help first-time landlords finish their taxes in an hour, and 140 already pay us every month." Use it as your email's first line and your first sentence in the room.

Getting the meeting

  1. Make a target list. Look for investors who have backed businesses at your stage and in your area. Don't pitch someone who never invests in your kind of business.
  2. Find a warm introduction. Ask a founder, mentor or teacher who knows them. A short note they can forward helps.
  3. Keep the first email short: your hook, three proof points, and one ask: a 20-minute call.
  4. Follow up with progress, not "just checking in". "Since we wrote, we added 30 customers" is a reason to reply.

In the meeting: make them want more

  • Open with the hook, then let them ask questions. Questions mean interest.
  • Ask them: "What would you need to see to invest?" Their answer is your to-do list.
  • End with a clear next step and date, and send a short thank-you note the same day.
Straight talk

Never fake interest from other investors or invent deadlines to create pressure. Investors talk to each other, and one lie can close every door. Real progress is the only pressure that works.

Try it

Write your one-line hook, then list five people who could introduce you to an investor or a mentor. Contact one this week.

Getting the best out of investors

Taking investment is like hiring a boss you can't easily fire. Choose carefully, agree fair terms, then put their help to work.

The 30-second version
  • You're choosing them too: check their reputation with founders they've backed.
  • Ask for specific help: introductions, hiring, advice.
  • Send short monthly updates, good news and bad.

Before you say yes: check them out

  • Talk to two founders they've backed, including one whose business struggled. How did the investor act when things went wrong?
  • Ask what help they give besides money, and for an example.
  • Read the terms with a lawyer. Money with bad terms can cost you more than no money (see Funding & valuation).

Questions to ask an investor

"How do you help after you invest? Give me a real example." "What happens if we miss our plan?" "How involved do you like to be?" "Who else could you introduce us to?"

After they invest: use them well

Ask forExample
Introductions"Could you introduce us to two people who buy for small landlords?"
Hiring help"Do you know a part-time bookkeeper we could hire?"
Advice on one decision"Should we raise prices now or after launch? Here are our numbers."

Specific asks get specific help. "Let me know if you can help" gets nothing.

The monthly update

Wins: 1 to 3, with numbers Numbers: sales, customers, cash, months of runway Problems: one honest one Asks: one specific thing they can help with

Send it every month, even in bad months. Investors who hear bad news early help. Investors who are surprised stop trusting you.

Straight talk

An investor's advice is input, not orders, unless your agreement says otherwise. Listen, thank them, then decide. It's your business, and you'll live with the result.

Write next month's update in the four lines above, even if you don't have investors yet. It's the fastest way to see what's working, and it becomes your first investor email.
