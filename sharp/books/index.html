Jim Collins · 2001

Good to Great

9/10 Must read

Read it for: Why a few companies go from good to great, and most never do. Based on years of research comparing companies that made the leap with similar ones that didn't.

The 6 big ideas, explained in our own words

  1. Level 5 leaders: humble but stubborn

    The leaders of the great companies weren't flashy. They cared more about the company winning than about looking good, and they never gave up.

    Example: Mia runs a small tutoring business. When a tutor gets a great review, she shares the credit. When a parent complains, she takes the blame and fixes it.

    Do this today: Next time something goes well, thank someone else by name.

  2. First who, then what

    Get the right people before you decide exactly where to go. Good people adapt when plans change; the wrong people slow everything down.

    Example: Jay wants a partner for his sneaker resale shop. He picks the friend who shows up on time and is honest, not the one with the most followers.

    Do this today: Write down the 3 qualities your first partner or helper must have.

  3. Face the brutal facts, but never lose hope

    Be completely honest about what's going wrong, and at the same time believe you'll win in the end. The book calls this the Stockdale Paradox.

    Example: Sales dropped for 3 weeks. Instead of pretending it's fine, Ana looks at the numbers, finds that her price went up, and tests a fix.

    Do this today: Write down the one fact about your business you've been avoiding.

  4. The Hedgehog Concept: three circles

    Find where three things overlap: what you can be the best at, what earns money, and what you really care about. Focus there and say no to the rest.

    Example: Leo is good at video editing, people pay for short edits, and he loves it. He drops logo design, which he's only average at.

    Do this today: Draw 3 circles: best at, makes money, love doing. What sits in the middle?

  5. A culture of discipline

    Great teams don't need lots of rules because the people are disciplined. Part of discipline is a "stop doing" list: things you quit to focus.

    Example: Sara stops answering every DM within minutes and checks messages twice a day, so she has time to make products.

    Do this today: Start a "stop doing" list with one thing on it.

  6. The flywheel

    Big results come from many small pushes in the same direction. No single moment changes everything; momentum builds slowly, then fast.

    Example: A bakery side hustle: post every week, deliver on time, ask for reviews. After 6 months, orders come in without asking.

    Do this today: Pick one small action you'll repeat every week for 3 months.

The main quotes, in plain English

  1. Top quote

    “Good is the enemy of great.”

    Chapter 1

    What it means: "Good enough" feels safe. That comfort is exactly what stops most people and businesses from becoming great.

    Try this: Pick one thing you do "good enough", like your product photos or how fast you reply. Make it great this week.

  2. “…first get the right people on the bus, the wrong people off the bus, and the right people in the right seats…”

    p. 41

    What it means: Who you build with matters more than your first plan. With the right people, you can fix a plan. With the wrong ones, no plan works.

    Try this: Before you pick a partner or first helper, ask: would I still want this person if our idea changed?

  3. “Level 5 leaders are a study in duality: modest and willful, humble and fearless.”

    p. 22–23

    What it means: The best leaders don't show off, and they never give up. Quiet and stubborn beats loud and flashy.

    Try this: When something goes well, credit others. When it goes badly, look at yourself first.

  4. “You can't manufacture passion or 'motivate' people to feel passionate.”

    p. 109

    What it means: You can't fake caring for long. Build around something you, and the people with you, actually care about.

    Try this: List 3 things you'd happily do for free. Does your business idea touch at least one?

  5. “They had freedom, but within a framework.”

    p. 123

    What it means: A few clear rules, plus freedom inside them, beats both bossing everyone around and having no rules at all.

    Try this: Write 3 rules for your business, like "reply to every customer within 24 hours". Decide everything else freely.

⚠ Be careful: Being great once doesn't last by itself. Circuit City, one of the book's great companies, went out of business in 2009, and Fannie Mae needed a government rescue in 2008. The habits have to continue.

Michael Masterson · 2008

Ready, Fire, Aim

8/10 Read it

Read it for: How to get a business from zero to real sales fast, and how your job as the founder changes as it grows.

The 5 big ideas, explained in our own words

  1. Businesses grow in stages

    The book describes four stages, from a brand-new business up to a big company. Each stage has different problems, and what worked in one stage can fail in the next.

    Example: A one-person phone-case shop needs sales above all. A 20-person company needs systems and managers.

    Do this today: Ask: "What stage am I in, and what is the main problem of that stage?"

  2. Stage one is about selling

    In the first stage, almost nothing matters more than learning to sell. Find the one way of selling that brings customers in at a profit, then do more of it.

    Example: Kai tries Instagram, flyers and asking neighbours. Neighbours bring the most paying customers, so he focuses there.

    Do this today: Try 2 ways to get customers this week and count which one brings sales.

  3. Fire before it's perfect

    Move from idea to action fast. A simple version in front of real customers teaches you more than months of planning.

    Example: Zara sells her first 10 bracelets from a basic photo on her phone instead of waiting for a perfect website.

    Do this today: Set a launch date within 7 days for the simplest version of your idea.

  4. Test small, then bet bigger

    Try ideas cheaply first. Decide in advance what result means "keep going", so you don't pour money into something that isn't working.

    Example: Before ordering 500 hoodies, Omar sells 20 pre-orders. He only orders more if 20 sell within 2 weeks.

    Do this today: Write down the result that would make you invest more, before you test.

  5. Your job changes as you grow

    At first you're mostly a salesperson. Later you become an inventor of new products, then a manager of people, and finally more like an investor.

    Example: Lily did every task in her lawn business. Once she hired two helpers, her job became training them and finding new customers.

    Do this today: List the tasks only you can do. Those are where your time should go.

The main quotes, in plain English

  1. Top quote

    “Nothing matters more than selling.”

    Stage one

    What it means: At the start, your number one job is getting paying customers. Not the logo, the business cards or the perfect website.

    Try this: This week, spend more time asking people to buy than on design.

  2. “The Ready, Fire, Aim concept is about velocity, about the profound benefits of moving from an idea into action…”

    p. 181

    What it means: Speed wins. Launch a simple version, then improve it using what real customers tell you.

    Try this: Launch a basic version of your idea within 7 days, even if it's small.

  3. “It's Ready, Fire, Aim, not Fire at Will.”

    p. 181

    What it means: Fast doesn't mean careless. Get ready first: know who buys, at what price, and how they'll hear about you. Then launch.

    Try this: Before you launch, write one line each: who buys, the price, and how they'll find you.

⚠ Be careful: It's written for building very big businesses; the subtitle is "Zero to $100 Million in No Time Flat". For you, "Fire" should mean a small, cheap test, not spending your savings.

T. Harv Eker · 2005

Secrets of the Millionaire Mind

6/10 Skim it

Read it for: Spotting your money habits and beliefs. The book is built around 17 "wealth files" that compare how rich and poor people think.

The 5 big ideas, explained in our own words

  1. Your money blueprint

    Your habits with money often come from what you heard, saw and went through as a kid. You can notice those beliefs and choose new ones.

    Example: Dev grew up hearing "money is the root of all evil" and feels guilty charging customers. Once he notices it, he raises his price.

    Do this today: Finish this sentence honestly: "Growing up, I learned that money is…"

  2. The money "thermostat"

    The book says people tend to drift back to the income they feel comfortable with, even after a big win. Growing your skills and habits helps the higher level stick.

    Example: Someone who gets $1,000 from a busy summer and spends it all in a month is back where they started.

    Do this today: Decide now where your next big payment will go, before it arrives.

  3. Act in spite of fear

    Fear isn't a sign to stop. Successful people feel it too; they just act anyway.

    Example: Nia is scared to ask her first customer for a review. She asks, and gets it.

    Do this today: Do one small thing today that scares you a bit, like posting your first offer.

  4. Get paid for results, not just hours

    If you're only paid per hour, your income has a ceiling. Being paid for results, like per project or per sale, can grow faster.

    Example: Instead of $15 an hour to edit videos, Leo charges $40 per finished video and gets faster at it.

    Do this today: Write one way you could charge per result instead of per hour.

  5. Split every dollar on purpose

    Manage money well by splitting each payment into set parts. The book suggests separate "jars" for things like financial freedom, savings, learning, needs, fun and giving.

    Example: From each $100 she earns, Ami puts $10 in savings, $10 into learning and $10 into her "freedom" account before spending.

    Do this today: Pick your percentages and split the next money you get.

The main quotes, in plain English

  1. Top quote

    “Rich people act in spite of fear. Poor people let fear stop them.”

    Wealth file 16

    What it means: Everyone feels scared before their first sales message or price increase. Feeling fear is normal; letting it decide for you is the problem.

    Try this: Send one message you've been putting off today.

  2. “Rich people focus on opportunities. Poor people focus on obstacles.”

    Wealth file 5

    What it means: See the problems, but spend most of your energy on what could work.

    Try this: For every problem you list about your idea, write one opportunity next to it.

  3. “Rich people focus on their net worth. Poor people focus on their working income.”

    Wealth file 13

    What it means: What you own minus what you owe matters more than any one paycheck.

    Try this: Work out your net worth: savings plus things worth money, minus any debts.

  4. “Rich people manage their money well. Poor people mismanage their money well.”

    Wealth file 14

    What it means: A joke with a point: being "good" at spending everything is still a skill, just aimed the wrong way.

    Try this: Track every dollar for one week with the budget planner.

  5. “Rich people constantly learn and grow. Poor people think they already know.”

    Wealth file 17

    What it means: The moment you think you know it all, you stop getting better.

    Try this: Do one Sharp lesson a day for a week.

⚠ Be careful: Splitting people into "rich" and "poor" thinkers is a big generalisation. Luck, family money and where you start matter too. The book also promotes the author's paid seminars. Keep the habits, skip the hype.

Napoleon Hill · 1937

Think and Grow Rich

6/10 Skim it

Read it for: Drive, persistence and having one clear goal. One of the best-selling success books ever written.

The 6 big ideas, explained in our own words

  1. A definite goal

    Hill says to decide exactly what you want, by when, and what you'll give in return, then write it down and read it often.

    Example: "I will earn $1,000 from my car-wash business by 1 August by washing 50 cars at $20 each."

    Do this today: Write your goal with an amount, a date and what you'll do to earn it.

  2. Burning desire

    Wanting something a little isn't enough. Strong desire is what keeps you going when it gets hard.

    Example: Sam really wants to pay for his own laptop, so he keeps knocking on doors after the first 10 people say no.

    Do this today: Write why your goal matters to you in one sentence. Put it where you'll see it.

  3. Specialised knowledge

    General knowledge doesn't make money by itself. Knowledge you organise into a plan does, and you don't need to know everything: you can learn it or ask people who do.

    Example: Rae doesn't know how to build a website, so she asks a friend who does and trades help with her designs.

    Do this today: List one thing you need to learn for your idea and one person who could teach you.

  4. Plan, and change the plan when it fails

    Make an organised plan. If it fails, the plan was wrong, not you. Make a new plan and keep going.

    Example: Kofi's flyers bring zero customers. He switches to asking for referrals from his first 3 clients.

    Do this today: After your next setback, change one part of your plan instead of quitting.

  5. Decide fast, persist long

    Hill noticed successful people decide quickly and change their minds slowly. Then they keep going long after others quit.

    Example: Lena spends one evening choosing between two ideas, picks one, and sticks with it for 3 months.

    Do this today: Make one decision today that you've been putting off.

  6. The Master Mind

    A small group of people who help each other toward their goals can go further than anyone alone.

    Example: Three friends with side hustles meet every Sunday to share numbers and swap advice.

    Do this today: Invite 1 or 2 people with goals to meet once a month.

The main quotes, in plain English

  1. Top quote

    “Do not wait; the time will never be 'just right.'”

    p. 127

    What it means: There is never a perfect time to start. Waiting for one is how ideas die.

    Try this: Set a start date for your idea within the next 7 days, and tell someone.

  2. “All achievement, all earned riches, have their beginning in an idea!”

    p. 18

    What it means: Every business starts as an idea. But an idea is only the start: it needs a plan and customers.

    Try this: Run your idea through the Idea check to see how many customers it needs.

  3. “Temporary defeat should mean only one thing, the certain knowledge that there is something wrong with your plan.”

    p. 73

    What it means: A failure means "fix the plan", not "quit".

    Try this: After your next "no", write down one change you'll make to your plan.

  4. “There is no substitute for persistence”

    p. 86

    What it means: Most people quit too early. Steady effort beats talent that gives up.

    Try this: Commit to 30 outreach messages before you judge whether your idea works.

  5. “Success comes through the application of power, and power is attained through the cooperative efforts of other people.”

    p. 87

    What it means: You go further with others. Hill called a group that helps each other a "Master Mind".

    Try this: Find 2 people with goals of their own. Meet once a month to share progress.

⚠ Be careful: Written in 1937. Parts of it claim that thoughts send out "vibrations" and that wanting something badly enough brings money. There's no evidence for that. Historians have also questioned some stories Hill told about his own life. Keep the persistence, skip the magic.

David Cameron Gikandi · 2001

A Happy Pocket Full of Money

3/10 Skip, or skim

Read it for: Feeling calmer and more grateful about money. It's a mindset book, not a business-skills book.

The 5 big ideas, explained in our own words

  1. Build value inside, then use it

    Become more valuable, through skills, honesty and effort, then put that value to work helping people. That's what people pay for.

    Example: Jo practises photo editing for a month, then offers edits to local shops.

    Do this today: Pick one skill to practise for 15 minutes a day this week.

  2. Gratitude and calm

    The book says feeling grateful for what you already have makes you calmer and less desperate. Calm people make better decisions about money.

    Example: Instead of panicking about a slow week, Ari notes what's going well and plans the next step.

    Do this today: Write 3 things that went right this week.

  3. Be clear about what you want

    Vague wishes create doubt. A clear goal makes the next step obvious.

    Example: "More money" becomes "$200 a month from dog walking by March".

    Do this today: Turn one vague wish into a goal with a number and a date.

  4. Give first

    Giving value before asking for anything builds trust, and trust leads to sales.

    Example: Mo answers questions in a local parents' group for a month. Then he offers tutoring and gets 4 students.

    Do this today: Help someone in your target market today without asking for anything.

  5. Live in the present

    Worrying about the past or the future drains your energy for today. Focus on what you can do now.

    Example: After a bad sales day, Pia stops replaying it and plans tomorrow's first task.

    Do this today: When you catch yourself worrying, write the next action you can take.

The main quotes, in plain English

  1. Top quote

    “To build external wealth, build internal value and then exercise it.”

    What it means: Make yourself more valuable, with skills, reliability and effort, then use that value to help people. That's what customers pay for.

    Try this: Pick one skill that would make your business better and practise it for 30 days.

  2. “Not clearly knowing what you wish to have is a major cause of doubt and disbelief.”

    What it means: Vague goals make you unsure. Clear goals make the next step obvious.

    Try this: Write one money goal with an amount, a date and how you'll earn it.

  3. “Give first before you receive.”

    What it means: In business, giving value first, like a free sample or a helpful answer, builds the trust that leads to sales.

    Try this: Help 3 people in your target market this week without asking for anything.

⚠ Be careful: Much of the book says your thoughts directly create money, using ideas it links to quantum physics. That isn't how physics, or money, works. Money comes from creating value that people pay for.

Robert B. Cialdini · 1984

Influence: The Psychology of Persuasion

9/10 Must read

Read it for: Why people say yes. Built on research and experiments, it explains six principles: give first (reciprocation), sticking to what you said (commitment and consistency), following others (social proof), liking, authority and scarcity.

The 6 big ideas, explained in our own words

  1. Reciprocation: give first

    People feel they should pay back what you give them. A real gift, like a free sample or useful advice, makes people more open to you.

    Example: A cookie stand gives a free bite-sized sample. More people buy a full cookie.

    Do this today: Offer one free, genuinely useful thing to possible customers this week.

  2. Commitment and consistency

    Once people say yes to something small, they like to stay consistent with it. Small first steps lead to bigger ones.

    Example: A tutor offers a $5 first lesson. Parents who say yes often sign up for more.

    Do this today: Design a small, easy first step customers can say yes to.

  3. Social proof

    When people are unsure, they copy what others like them are doing. Reviews, numbers and examples from similar people work.

    Example: "12 students from your school use my study guides" works better than "my guides are great".

    Do this today: Ask your 3 happiest customers for a short review you can share.

  4. Liking

    We say yes more to people we like: people who are similar to us, friendly, and on our side.

    Example: A shoe reseller who chats about the same teams and is easy to deal with gets repeat buyers.

    Do this today: In your next sale, find one real thing you have in common with the buyer.

  5. Authority

    People trust experts. Show real proof of your skill, like results, training or experience, never fake titles.

    Example: A math tutor shares that she got an A in the class and has helped 10 students improve.

    Do this today: Write one line of honest proof that you're good at what you sell.

  6. Scarcity

    Things seem more valuable when they're limited or running out. It only works honestly if it's true.

    Example: "I can only take 5 lawn customers this summer" is fine if it's true. A fake countdown is not.

    Do this today: If your time or stock is really limited, say so clearly.

The main quotes, in plain English

  1. Top quote

    “…we should try to repay, in kind, what another person has provided us.”

    Reciprocation

    What it means: People feel they owe you when you give first. A free sample or a genuinely helpful answer makes people want to give back.

    Try this: Give one real thing of value to a possible customer this week, like a tip or a sample, with no strings attached.

  2. “…as a rule, we most prefer to say yes to the requests of someone we know and like.”

    Liking

    What it means: People buy from people they like. Being friendly, honest and easy to deal with is part of selling.

    Try this: Before your next pitch, find one thing you genuinely have in common with the person.

  3. “…opportunities seem more valuable to us when their availability is limited.”

    Scarcity

    What it means: When something is rare or running out, people want it more. That's why "only 3 spots left" works, and why it must be true.

    Try this: If your time really is limited, say so honestly: "I can take 3 new clients this month."

⚠ Be careful: These six principles are exactly what scammers use too. Fake countdown timers, fake reviews and invented "only 2 left" claims win a sale once and cost you trust forever. Use them honestly or not at all.

Eliyahu M. Goldratt · 1984

The Goal

8/10 Read it

Read it for: Finding the one thing slowing your whole business down. It's written as a novel about a factory manager, so it reads like a story.

The 5 big ideas, explained in our own words

  1. The goal is to make money

    Being busy isn't the goal. The book measures progress with three numbers: throughput (money coming in from sales), inventory (money tied up in stuff you mean to sell) and operating expense (money spent to turn inventory into sales).

    Example: A T-shirt seller with 300 unsold shirts is busy but has money stuck in inventory.

    Do this today: Check how much of your money is stuck in unsold stock right now.

  2. The bottleneck sets your speed

    Every business has one slowest step. Your whole business can only go as fast as that step.

    Example: A slime business can make 50 tubs a day but only label 20. It sells 20.

    Do this today: Map your steps and find the slowest one.

  3. The slowest hiker (Herbie)

    In the book, a scout troop on a hike can only move as fast as its slowest kid, Herbie. The fix is to put Herbie at the front and lighten his backpack.

    Example: If packing is your slowest step, do packing first each day and get help with it, not with the fast steps.

    Do this today: Find one way to take load off your slowest step.

  4. Busy everywhere isn't efficient

    Making every step work flat out just builds piles of half-finished work in front of the bottleneck.

    Example: Baking 100 cakes when you can only decorate 30 means 70 cakes going stale.

    Do this today: Stop making more of anything that's waiting in a pile.

  5. Fix it, then look again

    Find the bottleneck, get the most out of it, make everything else work around it, then make it bigger. Once it's fixed, a new bottleneck appears, so repeat.

    Example: After the slime seller fixes labelling, delivery becomes the slowest step.

    Do this today: After you fix your slowest step, check again which step is now slowest.

The main quotes, in plain English

  1. Top quote

    “What you have learned is that the capacity of the plant is equal to the capacity of its bottlenecks”

    Jonah, the mentor

    What it means: Your business can only go as fast as its slowest step. If you can bake 100 cookies a day but only pack 40, you sell 40.

    Try this: Write down each step from "customer finds you" to "customer pays". Circle the slowest one. Fix that first.

  2. “An hour saved at a non-bottleneck is a mirage.”

    Jonah, the mentor

    What it means: Speeding up a step that isn't the slowest one changes nothing. It just feels productive.

    Try this: Next time you want to "work faster", check: is this the step holding everything else up?

  3. “So this is the goal: To make money by increasing net profit, while simultaneously increasing return on investment, and simultaneously increasing cash flow.”

    Alex, the manager

    What it means: Being busy isn't the goal. Making money is, and that means profit, a good return on what you put in, and cash coming in.

    Try this: For each task on your list, ask: does this help us make money? Drop one that doesn't.

⚠ Be careful: It's about factories, so some parts are slow if you're not into manufacturing. The idea works for any business, even a one-person side hustle.

Peter F. Drucker · 1967

The Effective Executive

9/10 Must read

Read it for: Getting the right things done. Five habits: know where your time goes, focus on results, build on strengths, do first things first and make good decisions.

The 6 big ideas, explained in our own words

  1. Know where your time goes

    Drucker says to record your time, cut what wastes it, and group the rest into big blocks. Important work needs long, unbroken time.

    Example: Ty finds 2 hours a day go to scrolling. He blocks 4–6pm for his business with his phone in another room.

    Do this today: Track your time for 3 days, then cut one waste.

  2. Focus on contribution

    Ask "What can I contribute that will really make a difference?" instead of "What am I busy with?"

    Example: Instead of redesigning her logo again, Kim asks what would help customers most: faster delivery.

    Do this today: Ask yourself the contribution question before you plan your week.

  3. Build on strengths

    Use what you and others are great at. Don't build a team around fixing weaknesses.

    Example: Jo is great at selling and weak at accounting, so she sells and pays a friend to do the books.

    Do this today: Write your top strength and make sure you use it daily.

  4. First things first

    Concentrate on the most important thing and do one thing at a time. Also decide what not to do; Drucker calls those "posteriorities".

    Example: Raj picks one product to perfect this month and puts 3 other ideas on hold.

    Do this today: Write today's one most important task. Do it first.

  5. Make few, good decisions

    Effective people make a small number of important decisions well. They want to hear disagreement before deciding, and ask whether a decision is even needed.

    Example: Before quitting her job for her business, Ava asks two people to argue against it.

    Do this today: For your next big decision, ask someone to argue the other side.

  6. Effectiveness can be learned

    Drucker says being effective isn't a talent you're born with. It's a set of habits you practise.

    Example: Nobody starts out organised; you build it one habit at a time.

    Do this today: Pick one habit from this list and practise it for a week.

The main quotes, in plain English

  1. Top quote

    “Effective executives do first things first and they do one thing at a time.”

    First things first

    What it means: Multitasking feels busy but gets less done. Pick the most important thing and finish it before starting the next.

    Try this: Each morning, write the one task that matters most today. Do it before you check messages.

  2. “Time is the scarcest resource, and unless it is managed nothing else can be managed.”

    Know thy time

    What it means: You can earn more money, but you can't get more hours. Wasted time is the most expensive waste there is.

    Try this: Log how you spend your time for 3 days. Circle what didn't help your goals.

  3. “Effective executives know where their time goes.”

    Know thy time

    What it means: Most people guess wrong about where their time goes. Measuring it is the first step to fixing it.

    Try this: Check your phone's screen time today. Is it where you'd want your hours to go?

  4. “Morale in an organization does not mean that 'people get along together'; the test is performance not conformance.”

    Staffing for strength

    What it means: A good team isn't one where everyone agrees. It's one that gets results, using what each person is best at.

    Try this: Write down what each person on your team, even a team of two, is best at. Give them more of that.

⚠ Be careful: It was written in the 1960s for managers in big companies, so the examples feel old. The habits still work for a one-person business.

Neville Goddard · 1944

Feeling Is the Secret

2/10 Skip it

Read it for: Learning to manage your mood and stay hopeful. It's a short spiritual book about "manifesting", not about business.

The 4 big ideas, explained in our own words

  1. Your mood changes your effort

    The book is right that going in feeling hopeless makes you try less. Expecting to fail often makes you quit early.

    Example: Before a pitch, Max reminds himself of his last good sale and speaks with more energy.

    Do this today: Before something hard, recall one past win.

  2. Self-control is a skill

    Learning to calm your feelings helps you make better choices, especially when things go wrong.

    Example: A customer leaves a rude review. Ella waits an hour, then replies politely and fixes the problem.

    Do this today: Practise box breathing once today: in 4, hold 4, out 4, hold 4.

  3. Picturing it isn't enough

    The book's main method is imagining your wish as already true. Research by psychologist Gabriele Oettingen found that only dreaming about success can actually lower effort. What works better is picturing the goal and then the obstacle in the way, and planning around it.

    Example: Instead of only imagining 100 sales, Kai pictures the goal, notes his obstacle ("I get nervous messaging people") and plans "I'll send 5 messages right after school".

    Do this today: Write your goal, your biggest obstacle, and "If [obstacle], then I will…".

  4. What the book gets wrong

    It says feelings alone create what happens to you and that you should ignore "the evidence of the senses". In business, ignoring real numbers is how you go broke.

    Example: Feeling like a millionaire doesn't pay for stock. Customers do.

    Do this today: Check one real number in your business today, like sales or costs.

Inner Excellence

6/10 Skim it

Read it for: Staying calm and focused under pressure. It's a mental-performance book used by pro athletes, and it's about mindset more than business.

The 5 big ideas, explained in our own words

  1. Fulfilment first

    Most people chase success to feel happy. The book argues it's the other way round: people who feel steady and fulfilled perform better.

    Example: Lia stops tying her mood to daily sales and enjoys the work, and her messages to customers get better.

    Do this today: Write what you enjoy about your work, separate from the money.

  2. Control the process, not the result

    You can't control every outcome, but you can control your effort and focus each day. Set goals for what you'll do, not just what you'll get.

    Example: "Send 10 messages a day" is in your control. "Get 10 customers" isn't fully.

    Do this today: Set one process goal for tomorrow.

  3. Less "me", less fear

    The book says worrying about how you look is one of the biggest obstacles. Focusing on helping others makes you braver.

    Example: Before a pitch, Ben thinks "How can I help this person?" instead of "What if I look dumb?"

    Do this today: Before your next scary task, write who it helps.

  4. Learn from discomfort

    Running from hard feelings stops you learning from them. Setbacks teach you if you're brave enough to look closely.

    Example: After a failed launch, Sana writes what went wrong instead of pretending it didn't happen.

    Do this today: After your next setback, write one lesson from it.

  5. Train your mind daily

    Athletes train their bodies every day; the book says your mind needs daily training too, with things like reflection and breathing.

    Example: Ten minutes each morning: breathe, plan the day, and pick one focus.

    Do this today: Try a 5-minute morning routine tomorrow.

The main quotes, in plain English

  1. Top quote

    “Most of us have had it backwards, trying to be successful in order to be happy.”

    What it means: Don't wait for success to feel okay. People who are steady inside tend to perform better, not worse.

    Try this: Write 3 things you're grateful for tonight, before checking how many sales you made.

  2. “The greatest freedom you have is where to place your thoughts.”

    What it means: You can't control every result, but you can choose what you focus on next.

    Try this: When you catch yourself worrying, ask: "What's the next small thing I can do?" Then do it.

  3. “Fear is one of the main barriers to learning from suffering.”

    What it means: Setbacks only teach you something if you're brave enough to look at them honestly.

    Try this: After a bad day, write what went wrong and one thing you'll do differently. No blaming.
